From first principles

How I would build the Great Pyramid

The starting point: suitable limestone lies close to the site, and labour and materials are unlimited. The available tools are copper chisels, stone pounders, wooden levers, sledges, rope, water, mudbrick, cord and plumb line. There are no wheels on sand, no pulleys and no iron. The plan below comes from the geometry and the physics, not from any particular theory.

2 · The fact that shapes the whole plan

A pyramid is mostly bottom. The volume below height h is 1 − (1 − h/H)³ of the total:

So I would build two different systems. One heavy, high-capacity ramp serves the lower 60 m, where four-fifths of the stone goes. A light, cheap ramp serves the remaining fifth. The last 0.1 % is lifted by hand with levers.

3 · Moving one block

The block goes on a wooden sledge, and the sledge is dragged over a track of timber sleepers bedded in packed clay. A man walks in front pouring water on the track. Wet clay roughly halves the friction compared with dry sand, to a coefficient of about 0.25. I assume a person can keep up a pull of about 350–400 N for hours.

Sixty blocks an hour is one block a minute. In the lower phase an average round trip is about 860 m up loaded, the same distance back empty, plus loading and handover. That takes roughly an hour, so about 60–65 teams must be on the move at any time, around 1,700 haulers.

5 · Step by step

Step 1 — Find north and lay out the square

Build a small circular wall with a perfectly level top to act as an artificial horizon. From its centre, mark where a bright star near the pole rises over the wall and where it sets. The bisector of those two marks points to true north. Peg out the 230 m square with stretched, pre-measured cords. Four equal sides and two equal diagonals guarantee right-angled corners. A 3-4-5 knotted cord checks each corner locally.

Step 2 — Level with water

Cut a grid of shallow trenches across the base and flood them. Water lies level, so a line scratched at the waterline on every trench wall gives the same height everywhere. Drain the trenches, cut the rock down to a fixed depth below the line, fill the trenches with stone and pave a flat platform. An outcrop in the middle can be left in place as part of the core, which saves stone.

Step 3 — Quarry and roads

Open the quarry a few hundred metres away and below the site. Crews free each block with copper chisels and pounders: they cut a channel around three sides, then split the base with wooden wedges. The waste chips are not wasted, because they become the fill for the ramps. A packed-clay road with timber sleepers runs from the quarry rim to the foot of the ramp. It is wetted constantly.

Step 4 — The lower 60 m: one wide straight ramp

The ramp starts at the middle of one face and runs straight out. It is 20 m wide at a 1 : 10 slope, built from quarry chips and mudbrick between retaining walls, with a sleeper track on top. Every time a course is finished, the ramp is raised by one course and lengthened by ten times that height. At 60 m it is about 600 m long and contains about 360,000 m³, roughly 14 % of the pyramid. It has three lanes: two for loaded sledges going up and one for empty sledges coming down. That gives about 90 teams an hour of capacity, comfortably above the 60 needed. The width also matters for the granite beams, which need hundreds of haulers on many parallel ropes.

Why not extend it to the top? A 1 : 10 ramp to 146 m would be about 1.5 km long and hold about 2.1 million m³, nearly as much as the pyramid itself, and it would be unstable at that height. It stops where it stops paying off.

Step 5 — Setting a course

  • On each new course, re-mark the centre from the course's own diagonals. The edge of the course is then set at (remaining height) × 5½ ⁄ 7 from the centre. Every course is measured from the centre rather than from the course below, so errors cannot pile up.
  • Lay the outer ring first: fine casing blocks with their outer faces left rough and oversized. Next lay the backing stones, then fill the core from the far side back towards the arrival point, so that sledges never have to cross freshly set stone.
  • Final positioning uses levers and a thin gypsum slurry, which works as a lubricant while the block is nudged into place. The top of the course is then dressed flat for the next one.

Step 6 — Chambers and granite

Passages and chambers are not dug out afterwards. They are built into the courses as the layers reach them, and simply left open. The granite beams of the King's Chamber, at about 43 m and above, arrive while the wide ramp is still in use. A 44 t beam needs about 400 haulers and a 65 t beam about 590.

Step 7 — 60 m to 132 m: a ramp wrapped around the stepped core

Above 60 m only a fifth of the stone remains, so a cheap ramp will do. Because the casing is still rough, each face is a staircase, and a ramp 6 m wide at a 1 : 8 slope can be built straight onto the steps. The pyramid carries it, so it needs only about 15,000 m³ of fill. It climbs around the faces about twice. At each corner a level landing gives room to lever the sledge through 90°. Loaded sledges use the inner lane and empty ones the outer lane. With one loaded lane and slow corners, capacity is about 25–30 blocks an hour. That is enough here, and it sets the pace of the upper phase.

Step 8 — The last 14 m and the pyramidion

Near the top the faces are too short for a ramp. The remaining stone is about 0.1 % of the total, a few thousand blocks. Each is raised by levering and cribbing: lift one end and slip in a timber shim, then lift the other end, and repeat. The block climbs the steps one at a time. The capstone (pyramidion) goes up in the same way and fixes the apex that all four faces must meet.

Step 9 — Finish from the top down

Starting at the apex, masons cut the rough casing faces back to one smooth plane. They stand on the steps and the ramp just below the finished line, so no scaffolding is ever needed. As the finished line moves down past each stretch of ramp, that stretch is dismantled. The last thing to disappear is the foot of the great ramp. Its fill is carted away or reused.

Unlimited labour does not make things faster beyond a point. The real limits are geometric: how many teams fit on a ramp, how many setting crews fit on a course, and how fast the shrinking upper courses can be supplied.

7 · What the animation simplifies

  • It uses 100 courses of 1.47 m instead of about 210 real courses of varying height.
  • Course blocks are drawn as a uniform grid about 1.6 m on a side. The hauled blocks are 1.1 × 0.9 × 1.25 m, which is about 2.8 t.
  • Only a representative number of teams is shown, and only part of the granite-beam crew.
  • Time is compressed and the haulers walk faster than real life. The order of events, the ramp geometry and the proportions are kept.
